STATE OF THE ART

From Saliency to Concepts, and Beyond

A review of the concept-based explainability landscape that frames TCAV and Visual-TCAV — the two central methods of this dissertation — within the field's progression: from the documented failures of pixel-level saliency, through supervised concept attribution, to automatic concept discovery, architectural integration, and causal concept reasoning.

01

How this review is organised

This chapter tries to give a faithful review of the current landscape of concept-based explainability methods, placing TCAV and Visual-TCAV within the broader taxonomy that spans from the foundational limitations of pixel-level attribution to the most recent innovations in concept discovery, concept-structured architectures, and causal concept reasoning.

The review is organised to reflect a roughly logical progression of the field: it goes through the shortcomings of saliency methods that motivated the turn toward concepts, through the supervised concept-based methods that define the core methodology used throughout this work, to the unsupervised and hybrid approaches that address the scalability limits of user-defined concept vocabularies — and finally to architectural and causal extensions that push concept-based reasoning beyond correlation.

Four axes recur throughout: whether concepts are user-defined or automatically discovered, whether the method yields local or global explanations, whether it is applied post-hoc or requires architectural modification, and whether the concept–prediction relationship is treated as correlational or causal. The comparison table in §06 situates every method surveyed here along these axes.

Pixel-level saliency

Gradient-based maps that identify which input pixels matter — and the documented instabilities (adversarial fragility, lack of input invariance, randomisation insensitivity) that motivate a move beyond pixels.

Supervised concept attribution

CAVs, TCAV, Regression Concept Vectors, Network Dissection and Visual-TCAV — probing activation space with human-defined concept directions. The methodological core of this dissertation.

Discovery, architecture & causality

ACE, CRAFT and ConceptSHAP automate concept discovery; Concept Bottleneck Models bake concepts into the architecture; CaCE and CRP push toward causal and spatially-grounded concept reasoning.

02

The limitations of saliency methods

Before examining concept-based approaches in detail, it is necessary to establish why they are needed. Gradient-based saliency methods are powerful and remain widely adopted, but a substantial body of work has revealed pathologies that limit their reliability as explanatory tools in critical fields — and it is precisely these pathologies that license the turn toward concepts.

Simonyan et al. · 2014
∂hc / ∂x — gradient of the
class score w.r.t. the input

[ The founding gradient-saliency paradigm ]

FOUNDATION

Saliency maps establish the gradient-based paradigm

Simonyan et al. (2014) introduced the foundational idea of computing the gradient of the class score with respect to the input image to produce saliency maps, establishing the paradigm of gradient-based attribution that dominated the field for several years.

Subsequent work, however, revealed fundamental instabilities. Ghorbani et al. (2018) demonstrated that adversarial perturbations — imperceptible to the human eye and leaving the predicted label unchanged — can produce dramatically different attribution maps, suggesting that saliency may reflect properties of the input–explanation mapping rather than stable properties of the model's decision process. Kindermans et al. (2017) showed that even a trivial constant shift of the input, with no effect on the prediction, can cause many attribution methods to produce entirely different importance maps; they formalised input invariance — the requirement that an explanation mirror the model's own sensitivity to such transformations — and showed that popular methods violate it. Dombrowski et al. (2019) extended these findings, linking the susceptibility of explanations to manipulation to specific geometric properties of neural networks and deriving upper bounds on how far attributions can be perturbed.

Adebayo et al. · 2018 · SmoothGrad · 2017
Randomised-network sanity checks
vs. noise-averaged gradients

[ Diagnosing what saliency actually reflects ]

SANITY CHECKS & NOISE

Randomisation tests and the limits of denoising

In a complementary line of work, Adebayo et al. (2018) observed that saliency maps produced by networks with randomly initialised weights are visually and quantitatively similar to those produced by fully trained networks — suggesting that many gradient-based explanations are dominated by the architecture's low-level edge detectors rather than by the learned decision function. SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al., 2017) attempted to address the visual noise in gradient maps by averaging over many noisy copies of the input; this improves perceptual sharpness, but does not resolve the underlying concern that the gradients themselves may not faithfully reflect the model's reasoning.

Sundararajan et al. · 2017
Path integral of gradients
from baseline $x'$ to input $x$

[ Completeness + sensitivity, axiomatically ]

AXIOMATIC APPROACH

Integrated Gradients — an axiomatic response

The axiomatic approach of Sundararajan et al. (2017) addressed some of these concerns by introducing Integrated Gradients (IG), which satisfies completeness (attributions sum to the difference between the prediction at the input and at a baseline) and sensitivity (a feature that changes the output receives non-zero attribution). IG's integration-along-a-path formulation is directly adopted by Visual-TCAV — generalised from pixel space to feature-map space — for computing concept attributions, as discussed in §03.

$$ \text{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \times \int_{\alpha=0}^{1} \frac{\partial f(x' + \alpha(x - x'))}{\partial x_i}\, d\alpha $$

Yet even IG operates at the pixel level, and by itself does not connect attributions to human-understandable concepts. These limitations — instability, architecture-dominance, and the absence of any semantic vocabulary — motivate the transition to concept-based methods: rather than attributing importance to individual pixels and hoping a human observer can infer which concept is responsible, concept-based methods define the concept explicitly and measure the model's sensitivity to it directly in activation space.

03

Supervised concept-based methods: from CAVs to Visual-TCAV

The supervised concept-based school of thought begins with the premise that a human analyst can define a concept of interest by providing a set of example images, and that the model's internal representations can then be probed to determine whether and how that concept influences its predictions. This section traces the methodological shift from the original TCAV through to its most recent spatial extension — the methods that form the experimental backbone of this dissertation.

Kim et al. · 2018
CAV = normal to a hyperplane
separating concept from random
examples in activation space

[ Linear probe, directional derivative ]

FOUNDATION

Concept Activation Vectors and TCAV

Kim et al. (2018) introduced the foundational framework for supervised concept-based explainability. A Concept Activation Vector (CAV) is the normal to a hyperplane separating activations of user-defined concept examples $C_p$ from random counterexamples $C_n$ at a given layer $l$ — formally, the weight vector $\mathbf{v}_C^l \in \mathbb{R}^m$ of a binary linear classifier trained on $\{f_l(x) : x \in C_p\}$ versus $\{f_l(x) : x \in C_n\}$.

The conceptual sensitivity of class $k$ to concept $C$ for input $x$ is the directional derivative of the class logit along the CAV direction, and the global TCAV score aggregates this over a class as the fraction of inputs pushed positively:

$$ S_{C,k,l}(x) = \nabla h_{l,k}(f_l(x)) \cdot \mathbf{v}_C^l, \qquad \text{TCAV}_{Q_{C,k,l}} = \frac{|\{x \in X_k : S_{C,k,l}(x) > 0\}|}{|X_k|} $$

Kim et al. validated the method by showing it correctly identified whether a model relied on visual features or on superimposed text captions (e.g. the word "cab" stamped on a cab image). TCAV is attractive because it requires little ML expertise, adapts to any user-defined concept, needs no retraining, and yields a single global quantitative score — but, by relying only on the sign of the directional derivative, it discards magnitude and provides no spatial granularity: it can say that "striped" matters for "zebra", but not where in the image. These are limitations of scope, not of correctness, and they motivate everything that follows.

Graziani et al. · 2019 · Bau et al. · 2017
Continuous concept regressors
vs. unit–concept IoU alignment

[ Two complementary global probes ]

CONTINUOUS CONCEPTS & A COMPLEMENTARY LENS

Regression Concept Vectors and Network Dissection

Graziani et al. (2019) extended the CAV framework with Regression Concept Vectors (RCVs), replacing the binary concept classifier with a linear regressor so that concepts can be defined on a continuous scale — useful in domains such as histopathology, where "degree of cell irregularity" is naturally graded rather than present/absent. RCVs widen the applicability of the framework but retain its global, spatially-ungrounded character.

Bau et al. (2017) took a different angle with Network Dissection: rather than probing for externally-defined concepts, it asks whether individual units already encode semantically meaningful categories, measuring the intersection-over-union between each unit's activation map and densely annotated concept masks (the Broden dataset). Where TCAV asks "is this concept important for this prediction?", Network Dissection asks "does any unit encode this concept at all?" — a concept may be encoded (high IoU) without being used (low TCAV score), or used through a distributed code that no single unit detects.

De Santis et al. · 2025
Pooled-CAV-weighted concept maps
+ Integrated Gradients in feature space

[ 98.33% concept-ranking accuracy ]

SPATIAL EXTENSION — THE METHOD AT THE CORE OF THIS WORK

Visual-TCAV: spatial grounding and per-instance attribution

De Santis et al. (2025) introduced Visual-TCAV to close TCAV's two principal gaps — the absence of spatial information and the inability to quantify per-instance ("local") concept importance. For each input it produces both a concept map that localises where the network recognises a concept, and a concept attribution score quantifying that concept's contribution to a class prediction.

The CAV is fitted as a difference of means between concept and random activations (motivated by Martin & Weller's finding that this is more stable than logistic-regression or SVM CAVs), then global-average-pooled into a pooled-CAV $p^c$. The concept map is a ReLU-gated, pooled-CAV-weighted sum of feature maps — structurally analogous to Grad-CAM, but concept-discriminative rather than class-discriminative, since it uses $p^c$ rather than pooled class-gradients as weights:

$$ M^c = \text{ReLU}\!\left( \sum_k p_k^c \cdot \text{fmaps}_k \right) $$

The concept attribution generalises Integrated Gradients from pixel space to feature-map space, integrating from a zero-filled baseline $F_0$ to the actual feature maps $F$ along a straight-line path, then masking by the (normalised) concept map. Designed to satisfy spatial grounding, per-instance attribution and aggregatability simultaneously, Visual-TCAV was validated on tagged images using the original TCAV protocol: it identified the more important concept with 98.33 % accuracy, against 71.67 % for Grad-CAM and 65.83 % for Integrated Gradients — differences confirmed significant by McNemar's test. This is the method whose PyTorch port and empirical behaviour are characterised in depth in the following two chapters.

04

Unsupervised and automatic concept extraction

The supervised methods above require the analyst to define concepts in advance and provide labelled examples. That requirement is natural in domains with a pre-existing conceptual vocabulary — the seven-point checklist in dermoscopy, used in this dissertation's clinical chapter, is exactly such a vocabulary — but it becomes a bottleneck when the goal is to discover what concepts a network has learned, rather than test for the presence of specific ones.

Ghorbani et al. · 2019 · ACE
Multi-resolution segmentation
→ activation clustering → TCAV

[ Automatic Concept-based Explanations ]

CLUSTERING-BASED DISCOVERY

ACE — Automatic Concept-based Explanations

Ghorbani et al. (2019) proposed ACE, which discovers concepts automatically by segmenting training images at multiple resolutions, extracting each segment's activations, and clustering them with $k$-means; each cluster becomes a "concept" whose importance is then estimated via TCAV scores from cluster-centroid CAVs. ACE removes the manual-definition bottleneck, but introduces new ones: discovered clusters are not guaranteed to be semantically meaningful, the number of clusters must be chosen, segmentation granularity strongly shapes which concepts emerge, and — because it scores concepts via TCAV — ACE inherits TCAV's global, sign-based limitation.

Fel et al. · 2023 · Zhang et al. · 2021
Non-negative matrix factorisation
+ Sobol importance + spatial maps

[ CRAFT, NCAV / ICE ]

FACTORISATION-BASED DISCOVERY

CRAFT and non-negative concept factorisation

Fel et al. (2023) addressed several of ACE's shortcomings with CRAFT, which applies Non-negative Matrix Factorisation to the activation tensor — the non-negativity constraint biases the decomposition toward parts-based, interpretable concepts. CRAFT contributes three innovations: a recursive strategy that detects concepts across multiple layers, a Sobol-index-based importance estimator that is more faithful than sign-counting, and Concept Attribution Maps derived through implicit differentiation that spatially ground the discovered concepts. In a related vein, Zhang et al. (2021) proposed Non-negative Concept Activation Vectors (NCAVs) within their Invertible Concept-based Explanations (ICE) framework, replacing the standard SVM-fitted CAV with a non-negative decomposition that is shown to be more faithful and more interpretable to human subjects than ordinary CAVs.

Yeh et al. · 2022 · ConceptSHAP
Completeness-optimised concepts
scored by Shapley value

[ Game-theoretic concept importance ]

COMPLETENESS & GAME THEORY

ConceptSHAP — completeness-aware explanations

Yeh et al. (2022) introduced the notion of completeness: the requirement that a concept set be sufficient, on its own, to account for the model's prediction behaviour. Their method, ConceptSHAP, discovers concepts by directly optimising for completeness while encouraging interpretability, and scores each discovered concept's importance with Shapley values computed over concept sets — a game-theoretic foundation that, unlike TCAV, can capture synergistic or antagonistic interactions between concepts rather than scoring each one in isolation.

05

Architectural integration, causal reasoning, and hybrid local–global methods

A final group of approaches moves beyond post-hoc analysis altogether — building concepts into the architecture, asking whether concepts are causes rather than correlates of predictions, or fusing the local and global perspectives that the methods above treat separately.

Koh et al. · 2020 · Mahinpei et al. · 2021
Input → concepts → class
two-stage, intervenable pipeline

[ Concept Bottleneck Models ]

ANTE-HOC ARCHITECTURE

Concept Bottleneck Models

Koh et al. (2020) proposed Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), which train the network to first predict human-defined concept labels from the input and only then predict the final class from those concepts. The explicit bottleneck makes concept predictions directly inspectable and intervenable — a user can correct a mispredicted concept at test time and watch the correction propagate to the final decision. CBMs reach competitive accuracy when concept annotations are dense and the vocabulary is comprehensive, but they are expensive to annotate and degrade when the concept set is incomplete. Mahinpei et al. (2021) further showed that CBM concept representations can encode information beyond the predefined vocabulary — information leakage — which can render downstream interpretations misleading.

Goyal et al. · 2020
VAE-mediated concept
intervention, do(concept)

[ Causal Concept Effect (CaCE) ]

CAUSAL, NOT JUST CORRELATIONAL

CaCE — Causal Concept Effect

Every method discussed so far establishes a correlational relationship: a high TCAV score means predictions correlate with a concept direction, not that the concept causes the prediction — a distinction that matters acutely in high-stakes applications. Goyal et al. (2020) introduced the Causal Concept Effect (CaCE), which measures the causal effect of a concept's presence or absence on predictions. Because directly intervening on activation space is intractable, they use a Variational Autoencoder to approximate the intervention, generating matched image pairs with and without the concept and measuring the resulting change in model output — in principle distinguishing genuine causal influence from a concept that is merely correlated with the predicted class because it co-occurs with other discriminative features.

Achtibat et al. · 2023 · Li / Chen et al.
Concept-conditioned LRP +
prototype / case-based reasoning

[ CRP, ProtoPNet ]

HYBRID LOCAL–GLOBAL & CASE-BASED REASONING

Concept Relevance Propagation and prototype networks

Achtibat et al. (2023) introduced Concept Relevance Propagation (CRP), which combines the local view of attribution methods with the global view of concept-based approaches by propagating relevance through Layer-wise Relevance Propagation rules conditioned on concept subspaces — yielding per-prediction concept-relevance maps, concept "atlases" of a model's learned representations, and concept-composition analyses. CRP occupies a position similar to Visual-TCAV — both pursue spatially-grounded, concept-level explanations — but arrives there through modified relevance-propagation rules rather than pooled-CAV-weighted maps and feature-space Integrated Gradients; a direct comparative evaluation between the two remains an open question.

A separate, case-based tradition draws on prototypes: Li et al. (2017) proposed a network with a prototype layer whose units resemble encoded training inputs, classifying by distance to these prototypes ("classified as X because its encoding is close to prototype Y"); Chen et al. (2019) extended this with ProtoPNet, which dissects an image into prototypical parts and combines part-level evidence — reasoning structurally close to how a domain expert might explain a decision, while matching the accuracy of non-interpretable counterparts.

Taxonomy of concept-based explainability methods

The landscape surveyed above can be organised along four axes: whether concepts are user-defined or automatically discovered, whether the explanation is local or global in scope, whether the method is applied post-hoc or requires architectural modification (ante-hoc), and whether it produces spatially-grounded output. 07

Summary and positioning of this work

TCAV and Visual-TCAV sit at a specific point in this taxonomy: supervised, concept-based, and — uniquely for Visual-TCAV — both globally aggregatable and spatially grounded. That combination is what makes them suitable for a clinical setting where a practitioner must be told not only which concept the model relied on but where in the image it found it. Yet the survey above also exposes exactly where the supervised, CAV-based family is fragile: CAV fitting depends on classifier choice and sample size, TCAV's sign-based score saturates and is symmetric under sign flips, and the negative reference pool against which a concept is contrasted silently determines what the resulting CAV actually measures.

Rather than take these properties on faith, this dissertation first interrogates them on a controlled synthetic benchmark — where the ground-truth concept that drives each classifier's decision is known by construction — before transferring the resulting methodological commitments to a real dermatological classifier with no such ground truth. Four questions drive that experimental programme, and the next two chapters answer them in turn:

  • Global attribution correctness — Can canonical Kim-2018 TCAV correctly rank concepts on a controlled benchmark where the true answer is known?
  • Spatial fidelity — Can Visual-TCAV spatially localise the right concept at the right place in the image?
  • Methodological robustness — Do the answers to the first two questions hold under every practitioner-controlled choice — random reference pool, CAV construction method, the activation space the CAV is fitted in?
  • Comparative advantage — What does concept-based Visual-TCAV offer that pixel-level saliency baselines (Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, LIME, Occlusion, …) do not?
