Why Concept-Based Explainability Matters

Modern deep learning architectures achieve remarkable predictive performance, yet their internal reasoning remains largely opaque, creating a growing and structural tension between capability and accountability.

01

Capability versus accountability

Deep learning systems are spreading across high-stakes domains, including medical image analysis, financial decision-making, autonomous systems and judicial sentencing assistance, which makes it increasingly necessary to understand their internal workings.

The field of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) emerges from the need to reconcile this tension. The absence of interpretability not only undermines user trust, but also impedes regulatory compliance, hinders model auditing, and limits the ability of practitioners to diagnose and correct systematic model failures.

Over the past decade the XAI community has converged around two principal schools of thought, ante-hoc and post-hoc explainability. Given the structural limitations of ante-hoc approaches, a significant proportion of contemporary research is directed toward post-hoc methodologies.

Ante-hoc explainability

Interpretability is a structural property of the model itself, achieved through constrained choices such as linear models, decision trees or human-readable attention mechanisms. Transparency by design tends to reduce representational capacity.

Post-hoc explainability

The trained model is treated as a fixed black box and external techniques approximate, attribute or decompose its behaviour after training. Its explanations are inherently approximate, and their fidelity is neither guaranteed nor always verifiable.

02

The post-hoc landscape

Post-hoc techniques follow a progression from input-level attribution to high-level semantic reasoning. Local methods explain individual predictions; global methods characterise behaviour across an entire class or dataset. Concept-based methods support both.

Perturbation-based

Alter, mask or remove input features and observe the change in output, treating the model as a strict black box. LIME and SHAP exemplify this, at the cost of many forward passes.

CAM-based

Project the final classification weights onto the last convolutional feature maps. Grad-CAM replaces the learned GAP weights with the gradient of the class logit.

Back-propagation

Compute the gradient of the output with respect to the input, giving pixel-wise saliency maps. Integrated Gradients addresses gradient saturation along a path from a baseline.

Explanation by example

Justify a decision by retrieving the most similar or influential training instances, leveraging the human tendency to reason by analogy and precedent.

Textual explanations

Translate model decisions into natural language, often with secondary or generative models, producing human-readable rationales alongside the prediction.

Concept-based

Elevate interpretability from pixels or tokens to semantic ideas, such as recognising the abstract concept of a “wheel” rather than a gradient of edge-detecting pixels.

03

A bridge between reasoning

Feature attribution remains trapped at the precision of the input. A pixel-level explanation may highlight a diffuse region as important, but it cannot say whether the model used a texture, a geometric pattern, or an incidental background correlation.

Kim et al. formalise this mismatch as a relationship between two vector spaces. The state of a model is a vector space $E_m$ spanned by basis vectors $e_m$ that correspond to input features and neural activations. Humans work in a different space $E_h$, spanned by implicit vectors $e_h$ that correspond to human-interpretable concepts. A dermatologist does not reason in pixel intensities but in the presence or absence of pigment networks, vascular patterns, regression areas and globules. An interpretation of a model is then a function:

$$ g : E_m \to E_h $$

The function $g$ need not be perfect. Its assumption of linearity draws on evidence from linear probing, network dissection and latent-space geometry that concepts are, at least locally and approximately, encoded as linear directions. Concept-based methods exploit this linearity by defining concepts through sets of example inputs: to define “striped texture”, a collection of images showing stripes is sufficient, which opens the process to domain experts without technical fluency.

Saliency methods ask

“Which pixels contributed to this prediction?”

Local and input-specific: a different map for each image, with no principled way to aggregate across instances.

Concept-based methods ask

“To what extent did the model rely on a human-understandable property?”

Global and class-level: whether predictions for a whole class are systematically sensitive to a concept, exposing biases, shortcuts and spurious correlations.

Two methods instantiate this framework. TCAV operationalises $g$ by training a linear classifier, a Concept Activation Vector, to separate the activations of concept examples from those of random counterexamples, then measures the directional sensitivity of the output along that vector. Visual-TCAV projects the concept vector back onto the spatial activation maps, showing not only whether a concept was used, but where the network detected it. Together they form the analytical backbone of this work.

04

Images and explainability

Images are simultaneously high-dimensional, spatially structured and amenable to human visual inspection: computationally complex yet perceptually immediate.

CNNs exploit this spatial structure through hierarchical convolutional filters. Early layers respond to edges, corners and colour gradients, while deeper layers compose them into detectors for textures, object parts and semantic categories. This hierarchy is what makes CNNs effective, and also what makes them difficult to interpret, as learned representations become progressively more entangled.

Image classification is therefore a natural setting for concept-based explainability: representations can be inspected at several levels of abstraction while the images remain directly accessible to human judgement.

05

Objectives

This final degree project evaluates the methodological validity and practical applicability of TCAV and Visual-TCAV for interpreting convolutional neural networks, using a two-phase experimental setup.

  • Controlled validation. Validate both methods under synthetic conditions with known ground truth, on the AIXI-Shape and TXUXI datasets (Chapter 3).
  • Clinical application. Apply them to a real-world dermoscopic skin lesion classification task, as V-MedCAV (Chapter 4).
